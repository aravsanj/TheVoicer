## Two Types of Words

Every sentence in English has two kinds of words working together.

**Content words** carry the meaning: nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs. These are the words that tell you *what* is happening.

**Function words** hold the grammar together: articles, prepositions, pronouns, conjunctions, and auxiliary verbs. These connect the content words but rarely carry new information on their own.

In spoken English, content words get **stressed**. Function words are usually **reduced**: spoken quickly and quietly, often with a schwa /ə/.

## Hearing the Pattern

The blanks below are function words. The content words are shown with their stressed syllable in bold. Fill in the blanks, then read the whole passage aloud.

> **Ja**ne **missed** ______ **train** ______ **ar**rived **late** ______ **work**. ______ **boss** ______ **fu**rious. ______ ______ **rushed** ______ ______ **door** ______ ______ **break**fast ______ **for**got ______ **badge** ______ ______ **ta**ble.

**Answers:** her / and / to / Her / was / She / had / out / the / without / her / and / her / on / the

Notice how the content words (*Jane, missed, train, arrived, late, work, boss, furious, rushed, door, breakfast, forgot, badge, table*) all land on a beat. The function words fill the gaps between them but don't carry the rhythm.

If you stressed every word equally, the sentence would sound flat and robotic. The stress pattern *is* the rhythm of English.

## Function Words Have Two Forms

Most function words have a **stressed form** and an **unstressed form**. The phonetic mechanism behind this is vowel reduction to /ə/, covered in [Reduced Vowels in Unstressed Syllables](/lessons/reduced-vowels-schwa). In ordinary speech, the unstressed form is the default. The stressed form appears when you want to emphasize contrast, add focus, or correct a misunderstanding.

| Word | Stressed | Unstressed | Example (unstressed) |
|------|----------|------------|----------------------|
| a | /eɪ/ | /ə/ | She took a break. |
| an | /æn/ | /ən/ or /n/ | Give me an answer. |
| and | /ænd/ | /ənd/ or /n/ | Grab a coat and umbrella. |
| are | /ɑr/ | /ɚ/ | The keys are on the table. |
| as | /æz/ | /əz/ | It's not as hard as I thought. |
| because | /bɪˈkɔz/ | /bɪkəz/ | I left because it was late. |
| can | /kæn/ | /kən/ | I can do it. |

The stressed form is the one you'd find in a dictionary. The unstressed form is what you actually hear in most sentences.

## Stressing "can": Two Different Meanings

The word *can* shows how stress on a function word shifts meaning.

1. **~~She~~ can **drive**.** ("Can" is stressed; "she" is reduced.)
   = She has the ability or permission to drive.

2. **~~She can~~ **drive**.** ("Can" is unstressed; "drive" carries the stress.)
   = Driving is one thing she's able to do.

In sentence 1, the point is her ability. In sentence 2, the point is the specific skill.

## When Stress on a Function Word Changes the Sentence

The same sentence can carry a different meaning depending on whether a function word is stressed or unstressed.

| Form | Sentence | Meaning |
|------|----------|---------|
| /n/ (unstressed) | **Tom** ~~and~~ **Lisa** both **passed**. | Simple information about two people. |
| /ænd/ (stressed) | **Tom** and **Lisa** both **passed**. | Lisa passing too was unexpected. |
| /ɚ/ (unstressed) | The **results** ~~are~~ **in**. | Straightforward announcement. |
| /ɑr/ (stressed) | The **results** are **in**. | Emphasis: they really are in now. |

The unstressed form carries the grammar. The stressed form adds surprise, contrast, or correction.

## Listening for the Rhythm

When you listen to spoken English, train yourself to hear which words get the stress. The stressed words will be louder, longer, and higher in pitch. Everything between them moves quickly.

When you speak, don't try to stress every word. Let the function words reduce. The content words will stand out on their own.
